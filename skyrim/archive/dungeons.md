# Excavating a Nordic Barrow — Dungeon Picks & Lesson Plan

*Splash 2026 · 1 hour · companion to `intro-slides.tex`*

---

## Suggested run of show (60 min)

| Time | Segment | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|
| 0–5 | Hook | Put the class blurb on screen. Ask: *"What's the difference between a dungeon and an archaeological site?"* (Answer to aim for: nothing, except who's holding the torch and why.) |
| 5–17 | Brief history of Skyrim | `intro-slides.tex`. Keep it brisk — just enough to date what we find. |
| 17–20 | The archaeologist's toolkit | Last slides of the deck: superposition, *terminus post quem*, reuse, disturbance. |
| 20–50 | Live excavation | Walk through **Bleak Falls Barrow** (or your pick below), layer by layer. Pause at each layer for the three questions. |
| 50–58 | Synthesis | Reconstruct the site's biography as a class on the board, from oldest to newest. |
| 58–60 | Exit question | *"If someone excavated your bedroom in 1,000 years, what layer would they misread?"* |

**The three questions to repeat at every layer** (straight from the blurb):

1. **Who built this, and for whom?**
2. **What is it meant to convey, and how does the design show that?**
3. **What has happened to it since?**

## Main pick: Bleak Falls Barrow

**Why this one:** It's the first barrow most players see, it's right above Riverwood, and it is *beautifully stratified* — the recent junk is on top and the old stuff is literally deeper. It also comes with a backstory about theft, a museum-like artifact (the Dragonstone), and a "key" (the Golden Claw) that only works if you read the iconography.

### Layer-by-layer

**Layer 0 — The approach and exterior (surface survey)**
- What you see: huge stone arches on a mountainside, dragon-like curved stonework, a commanding view over the valley.
- *Ask:* Why build here? (Visibility, sacred height, controlling the road, being seen by the living.) What does the scale say about who was buried here?
- *Disturbance:* Bandit camp and watchtower on the outside — the site is being reused as a fortification.

**Layer 1 — Upper halls (modern occupation)**
- What you find: bandits, bedrolls, campfires, food, Septim coins, a pressure-plate trap.
- *Teaching point — terminus post quem:* A Septim coin can't have been dropped before the Septim Empire minted it, so this layer is **no older than the Third Era**. The ancient Nords didn't use Septims.
- *Teaching point — reuse:* Ruins become shelters. Archaeologists find this constantly (squatters in Roman villas, medieval homes inside amphitheaters).

**Layer 2 — The rotating pillar puzzle**
- What you find: rotating pillars carved with animal glyphs that must be set to match the glyphs carved nearby (the answer is in the room).
- *Ask:* Who was this lock meant to keep out, and who was meant to get through? (People who could *read* the iconography — insiders, priests.) What does it mean that the answer is sitting right there on the wall?

**Layer 3 — Spider nest & Arvel the Swift (recent disturbance)**
- What you find: a Dunmer thief trapped in webs, carrying the Golden Claw he stole from Lucan's shop in Riverwood.
- *Teaching point — looting and the antiquities trade:* The Claw left the site, was sold or displayed in a shop, was stolen, and came back. A real artifact's *provenance* is often just this messy. Also: Arvel's journal is a primary source about the site — written by a looter.
- *Bioturbation:* Skeevers and frostbite spiders are the Skyrim version of burrowing animals messing up the deposits.

**Layer 4 — Burial halls (the original deposit)**
- What you find: draugr in wall alcoves, sarcophagi, burial urns, grave goods, Nordic knotwork.
- *Ask:* Who gets an alcove and who gets a sarcophagus? What does that hierarchy tell us about the society? Why are the dead armed?
- *Ask:* Draugr wake when you enter. Is this tomb meant to keep intruders **out**, or the dead **in**?

**Layer 5 — The claw door**
- What you find: a door with three rotating rings and a claw-shaped keyhole; the answer is engraved on the palm of the Golden Claw.
- *Teaching point:* The key and the lock were made together as one system. Reading the object (turning the claw over) is the archaeological skill that opens the door.

**Layer 6 — The sanctum: Word Wall and the Dragonstone**
- What you find: a Word Wall in the dragon language and the Dragonstone, a stone map of dragon burial sites.
- *Ask:* The deepest, most protected thing in the tomb is *text* and a *map*. What did the builders value most? Why would a burial place protect information about dragons?
- *Connection:* Farengar (the Jarl's court wizard) wants the Dragonstone for research — an institution claiming an artifact. Fair? Compare with real debates over museums and where artifacts belong.

### Site biography (build this on the board at the end)
1. Built by the Dragon Cult era Nords → burial site + repository of dragon knowledge.
2. Sealed, guarded by draugr and puzzles.
3. Centuries of abandonment; skeevers and spiders move in.
4. Golden Claw removed at some point and ends up in a Riverwood shop.
5. Arvel steals it back and dies trying to use it.
6. Bandits occupy the upper levels.
7. *You* arrive — and you are also a disturbance.

## Alternatives & extras (pick one if you want variety, or use them as quick comparisons)

| Dungeon | Hold | Best for teaching | The hook |
|---------|------|-------------------|----------|
| **Saarthal** | Winterhold | Actual archaeology; the oldest layer | The College of Winterhold is running a literal excavation with Tolfdir as dig supervisor. It's an Atmoran site from the time of the Night of Tears, the deepest root of Nord history. Good questions about who gets to dig and what happens when you find something you don't understand (the Eye of Magnus). |
| **Forelhost** | The Rift | Reading a site's last days | A Dragon Cult fortress-monastery that was besieged after the Dragon War. The defenders poisoned their own water rather than surrender. Journals and the layout let you reconstruct the siege from the inside. Great for "what happened to this place?" |
| **Korvanjund** | The Pale | Heritage as politics | The Stormcloaks and the Empire race to grab the Jagged Crown, a crown of ancient High Kings, because owning the past means a claim to rule now. Directly hits the "still-sodden battlefields" line in your blurb. |
| **Ustengrav** | Hjaalmarch | Looting before you get there | A tomb in a bog, holding Jurgen Windcaller, founder of the Way of the Voice. You get to the end and the Horn is already gone, with only a note in its place. A perfect gut-punch about looters reaching a site first. Necromancers have also moved in. |
| **Dead Men's Respite** | Haafingar | Texts vs. material evidence | The tomb of King Olaf One-Eye. Olaf suppressed a bard's unflattering verse about him; the ghost of that bard (Svaknir) and the recovered verse tell another story than the official one. A great way to discuss how the written record can lie. |
| **Ysgramor's Tomb** | Winterhold | Living heritage | The Companions still use and guard it, so it's an active religious site. Compare with a ruin nobody remembers. Who is the "descendant community" here? |
| **Yngol Barrow** | Winterhold coast | Memorials without bodies | Built by Ysgramor for his son, who died at sea. Small, easy to show, good for "who is a tomb really for: the dead or the grieving?" |
| **Folgunthur / Geirmund's Hall** | Hjaalmarch / The Rift | Tombs as prisons | The Gauldur story: three sons murdered their father and were sealed in separate tombs to keep them apart. Folgunthur even has a Dunmer explorer (Daynas Valen) leaving notes. Burial can be a punishment, not an honor. |
| **Labyrinthian (Bromjunaar)** | Hjaalmarch | Many layers stacked up | The Dragon Cult capital, later rebuilt by the wizard Shalidor, then home to the Staff of Magnus quest and the dragon priest Morokei. The clearest example of reuse across eras, but it's long, so show it in screenshots. |

### If you only have time to *show* one alternative
Use **Ustengrav's empty pedestal** or **Korvanjund's crown**. Both take about 2 minutes to explain and both land hard.

## Archaeology concepts → Skyrim examples (cheat sheet)

| Concept | One-line definition | Skyrim example |
|---------|--------------------|----------------|
| Law of superposition | Lower layers are usually older | Bandits on top, draugr below, Word Wall deepest |
| *Terminus post quem* | A layer is no older than the newest thing in it | Septim coins mean Third Era or later |
| Reuse | Later people reoccupy old sites | Bandits, necromancers, Shalidor at Labyrinthian |
| Disturbance / bioturbation | Animals and people mix up the deposits | Skeevers, spiders, Arvel |
| Looting & provenance | Objects leave sites, and their history gets murky | The Golden Claw; the missing Horn of Jurgen Windcaller |
| Grave goods & status | Burial treatment shows social hierarchy | Alcoves vs. sarcophagi vs. the dragon priest's crypt |
| Text vs. material record | Writing can mislead; objects can correct it | Dead Men's Respite and Olaf's poem |
| Contested heritage | Who owns the past, and who uses it? | The Jagged Crown; Farengar vs. the site |
| Descendant communities | Living people tied to the dead | The Companions at Ysgramor's Tomb; the Greybeards |

## Practical prep checklist

- [ ] Save files placed at the entrance of your chosen barrow (and one inside each major layer, so you can jump if you're short on time).
- [ ] Consider using `tgm` / `tcl` (god mode / noclip) in the PC console so combat doesn't eat class time. Turning up the brightness also helps on a projector.
- [ ] Screenshot backups of each layer in case the game or projector fails.
- [ ] Optional: let a student "drive" during one layer while the class directs them like a dig team.
- [ ] Have Arvel's journal and the Golden Claw's palm ready to read aloud; primary sources are the fun part.

> **Lore caveat:** Names and broad events here follow the game, but exact in-universe dates are often fuzzy or contradictory across the series. If a student catches one, that's a great moment to talk about how real ancient chronologies are built from inconsistent sources too.
